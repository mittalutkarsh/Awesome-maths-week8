The plan

One way of thinking for every problem this week, in four steps. One running example carries through all of them: 3, __, __, 24, … Find the sixth term and the sum of six terms.

  1. Default back to a, ar, ar², … Put each known term on its rung. (A repeating block is one rung.)
  2. Divide two rungs to get the power of r you know. Don't solve for r unless you must.
  3. Build what you need from that power: a term, or a sum from a, r and the next rung r^(n+1). Forever with −1 < r < 1: the next rung → 0.
  4. Check: did an even power hide a ±? List the first few rungs.

Forever sums

When −1 < r < 1 the next rung shrinks to 0, so the finite formula becomes a ÷ (1 − r).

From class. Zeno's paradox (57:04): positions ½, ¾, ⅞, 15/16, 31/32, …; the gap (½)ⁿ → 0, so the sum approaches 1. Board example (69:30): 1/7 + 1/49 + ⋯ = (1/7) ÷ (6/7) = 1/6.

More worked numbers. 0.6 + 0.06 + 0.006 + ⋯ = 0.6 ÷ 0.9 = 2/3. 5 − 5/2 + 5/4 − ⋯ (r = −½) = 5 ÷ 3/2 = 10/3.

One method, every problem

It all rests on the teacher's opening idea: a geometric sequence is defined by multiplication, and repeated multiplication is a power. So every term is a rung a·rk, two rungs divide to a power of r, and the power you need is built from the power you know. Four questions do every problem this week. Tricky bits

Each trap comes from a real problem in the set, with the tempting wrong answer.

  • Adding instead of multiplying (Problem 1)
    1, 2, 3 Adding 1 each time is arithmetic. “Geometric” means multiply.
  • The average isn't the geometric middle (Problem 2)
    185 (37 + 333) ÷ 2 is the arithmetic middle. Rungs multiply, so divide instead.
  • Forgetting the ± after an even power (Problem 2)
    only 111 r² = 9 allows r = 3 and r = −3, so there are two second terms.
  • Hunting for r when you only need r⁴ (Problem 3)
    r = ⁴√(−2)? No real r has r⁴ = −2. The 13th term needs only (r⁴)³.
  • Using the last rung instead of the next (Problem 4)
    2047 The formula needs r^(n+1), the power one rung past 2048.
  • Last term in place of the power (Problem 5)
    9110 5(1 − 3645) ÷ (1 − 3) puts the last term, a·rⁿ, where r^(n+1) belongs.
  • Reading the denominator as the count (Problem 6)
    2048 terms 1/2048 = (½)¹¹: eleven rungs, not 2048.
  • Dropping the first term (Problem 8)
    4/3 1 ÷ (1 − ¼) uses a = 1. The series starts at ¼.
  • One ratio when the ratios alternate (Problem 9)
    1/5 ×2/7 then ×1/14: bundle pairs into blocks with ratio 1/49.
  • Only half the terms (Problem 10)
    4/3 1 + ¼ + 1/16 + ⋯ leaves out the √2 terms.
  • Ratio 1 in a forever sum (Problem 12)
    r = 1 Every rung would be a, and the sum grows forever.
  • Skipping the forever check (Problem 13)
    any r Both ratios, r and 2r, must be between −1 and 1. 2r = 22/23 passes.
  • Dividing 1 instead of the second term (Problem 14)
    (C) a = 2 ÷ r, not 1 ÷ r.
  • Treating blocks as equal steps (Problem 19)
    560 Blocks multiply by 0.9 each time; they don't repeat 180.
  • Forcing the ladder onto an arithmetic problem (Problem 21)
    a ratio Problem 21 adds a fixed difference: it's last week's idea.

Cheat sheet

SituationMove
Two terms given, one wantedPut both on rungs, divide to a power of r, build the wanted power.
An even power of r appearsKeep both signs, unless the wanted rung uses an even power too.
A power you can't undo (r⁴ = −2)Don't find r. Build the wanted power from the known one.
A finite sumFind a, find r, find the next rung r^(n+1) = r × (last ÷ a). Then a(1 − r^(n+1)) ÷ (1 − r).
A forever sumCheck −1 < r < 1 first. Then a ÷ (1 − r).
Ratios that alternate or repeatBundle the repeating block into one rung; block ratio = r^(block size).
Sums of long blocks (AIME 2011)Each block is the one before × r^(block length).
Build a sequence with given whole numbersChoose a and r: factors of r's denominator in a decide how many rungs stay whole.
Differences of terms givenFactor each difference (ar³(r − 1), a(r − 1)) and divide them.
Terms add to somethingWrite them as a, ar, ar² and factor out a.

Glossary

Geometric sequence
A list where each term is the one before times the same number.
Common ratio, r
That number. Find it by dividing a term by the one before it.
Rung
Our picture word for one term of the ladder; the k-th rung is a·r^(k−1).
a₀ or a₁?
If the first term is called a₁, term k is a·r^(k−1). If it's called a₀, term n is a·rⁿ. The lecture note's list a, ar, …, arⁿ has n + 1 terms.
Geometric series
The sum of the terms of a geometric sequence.
Next rung, r^(n+1)
The power of r one rung past the last term. The sum formula needs it, not n.
Infinite series “approaches” L
The running totals get as close to L as you like. Writing … = L is a shorthand for that.
Even power
r², r⁴, r⁶, …: always positive (or 0) for a real r, so the sign of r is hidden.
Geometric mean
The middle term of three positive rungs: √(xy). For 4, 12, 36 it's 12.
Complex number
A number outside the real number line. Needed for r⁴ = −2 (Problem 3) and r² + r + 1 = 0 (Problem 20).
Relatively prime
Sharing no factor bigger than 1, like 11 and 23.
If and only if
Both directions: whole when i ≤ 2015, and not whole when i > 2015.

Write-up habits

  1. Write the form a, ar, ar², … first and mark the rungs you know (15:00, “when in doubt, default back”).
  2. Name a, r and n + 1 before using the sum formula (39:30, 41:04).
  3. Don't compute n unless you need it: r^(n+1) = r × rⁿ (53:33).
  4. When an even power is undone, write both answers (17:01).
  5. Simplify radicals in final answers: 37√27 becomes 111√3 (27:35).
  6. Circle the condition −1 < r < 1 before using a ÷ (1 − r) (68:02).
  7. Check a small case by direct addition (40:31: 4 + 12 + 36 + 108 = 160).
  8. Compute without adding extra work beyond what the problem needs (47:10).
